# Python Community Admin Meeting and 30-Day Pilot Playbook

## Document Purpose

This document consolidates the complete preparation, facilitation, decision-making, execution, measurement, and
review framework developed for the Python WhatsApp community admin meeting.

It is designed for two uses:

1. A live guide for facilitating the 75-minute admin meeting.
2. A durable operating record for the resulting 30-day manual pilot.

The meeting should convert a broad concern about admin inactivity and single-person dependency into a limited,
measurable, and explicitly owned operating experiment. It should not attempt to solve every community problem,
launch every proposed activity, or design automation before the manual processes are understood.

## Source Context

### Current Situation

- Uvais reported that the community is becoming difficult to sustain because admin participation is insufficient.
- The membership and management system requires recurring work, but too much of that work currently depends on
  Uvais.
- Uvais cannot manage the full membership and administration workload alone.
- Working admins may have limited availability, but the community still requires consistent shared support.
- The initial options raised were increased participation from existing admins or replacement by people with more
  available time.
- The discussion then moved from judging individuals to defining the system: required work, minimum responsibilities,
  SOPs, ownership, manual testing, measurement, and eventual automation.
- Uvais indicated that approximately 20 minutes of manageable work is acceptable to him and strongly supported
  automation where it produces a worthwhile return.
- Human involvement should eventually focus on judgment, approvals, exceptions, and sensitive decisions.

### Agreed Four-Phase Direction

1. Discover the administrative, engagement, learning, governance, and measurement work required.
2. Plan the work, document SOPs, execute it manually, and measure the results.
3. Build full or partial automation only for processes validated through manual execution, retaining human gates.
4. Operate the pipelines, measure outcomes, improve them, and stop work that does not justify its cost.

The immediate focus is Phase 1 and Phase 2. Automation belongs in a backlog until the relevant manual process is
stable, repeatable, valuable, and understood.

## Meeting Success Definition

The meeting is successful when every attendee can answer these seven questions without ambiguity:

1. Why does the community exist during the next three months?
2. What exact work will be performed during the next 30 days?
3. Who owns and backs up each selected activity?
4. When will each activity and deliverable happen?
5. How much admin effort is available and expected?
6. What evidence will determine whether each activity continues, changes, or stops?
7. When will the evidence be reviewed and the next decisions made?

The desired result is not the largest list of ideas. It is shared facts, constrained scope, accepted ownership,
explicit dates, measurable outcomes, and a closed review loop.

> small 30-day pilot. We will document decisions and action items during the meeting. Automation will be discussed
> only for activities that we first agree to test manually.

### Meeting Objective to Display

> Agree on a sustainable 30-day pilot for operating the Python community, with defined activities, owners, time
> commitments, measurements, and a review date.

## Meeting Ground Rules

1. Discuss observable problems and processes rather than blaming individuals.
2. One person speaks at a time.
3. Every selected activity has one accountable owner.
4. "All admins" and "anyone available" are not valid ownership assignments.
5. Commitments are based on reliable minimum capacity, not ideal availability.
6. The 30-day pilot is reversible and does not create permanent obligations.
7. Useful but out-of-scope ideas go into the backlog.
8. Automation ideas go into the automation backlog until manual validation is complete.
9. Decisions, non-decisions, objections, owners, dates, and reasons are written during the meeting.
10. Silence is not treated as acceptance of an assignment.

## Stage 2: Hear Each Admin's Perspective and Capacity

### Purpose

Collect facts before assigning work. The objective is not to measure who cares most. It is to understand what each
admin observes, can contribute reliably, prefers to handle, and cannot currently handle.

### Round-Robin Format

Give each admin approximately two uninterrupted minutes. Ask every person the same five questions:

1. What is the most important problem you currently see?
2. What is one thing the community is already doing well?
3. What should members receive that they are not receiving now?
4. How much time can you reliably contribute each week for the next 30 days?
5. Which responsibilities can you own, support, or not currently handle?

### Problem Diagnostic Questions

1. What recent example demonstrates the problem?
2. How frequently does it occur?
3. Who is affected?
4. What happens if nothing changes for three months?
5. Is it mainly a member, admin, process, access, capacity, skill, or technology problem?
6. Is ownership explicit or informally assumed?
7. Where does work currently accumulate?
8. What currently depends only on Uvais?
9. What stops when a particular admin is unavailable?
10. Which work produces frequent interruptions or stress?
11. Which activities consume effort without visible value?
12. What expectations do members currently have?
13. Have admins promised activities that were not delivered?
14. Are there repeated member complaints or unresolved incidents?

### Capacity Questions

1. What is your reliable minimum contribution, rather than your ideal contribution?
2. Can you contribute on fixed days or only when available?
3. Are weekdays or weekends more realistic?
4. Do you prefer one longer block or several short tasks?
5. Can you handle recurring work?
6. Can you handle occasional events but not recurring work?
7. Can you respond to urgent moderation issues?
8. What routine response time can you sustain?
9. Will your availability change during the next 30 days?
10. Are there known work, study, family, or travel constraints?
11. Do you prefer public member interaction or behind-the-scenes work?
12. Do you prefer moderation, writing, speaking, coordination, development, documentation, or analysis?
13. What could you continue reliably for three months?
14. What would probably become unsustainable?
15. Can you serve as a backup without becoming the primary owner?
16. Do you need training, access, templates, or an SOP before accepting responsibility?

## Stage 4: Prioritize Essential Work

### Purpose

Reduce the inventory to a pilot that fits confirmed capacity and produces useful evidence.

### Prioritization Gates

Before scoring, ask:

1. Does the activity support the agreed purpose?
2. Is someone realistically available to own it?

If either answer is no, put the activity in the backlog regardless of score.

### Scoring Model

Score each factor from 1 to 5. Use `effort feasibility`, where a higher score means easier and more sustainable.

| Factor | Score 1 | Score 3 | Score 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Member value | Little visible benefit | Useful to a segment | Strong direct benefit to many |
| Urgency | Can wait several months | Should happen this month | Immediate safety or operating need |
| Effort feasibility | High recurring effort | Manageable effort | Low effort and easy delivery |
| Automation potential | Rare or judgment-heavy | Partially repeatable | Frequent, structured, and repeatable |

Use this formula:

```text
Priority score =
(Member value x 3)
+ (Urgency x 3)
+ (Effort feasibility x 2)
+ (Automation potential x 1)
```

Member value and urgency receive the greatest weight. Do not select an activity merely because it is easy to
automate.

### Discussion Questions

1. What harm occurs if this activity is omitted?
2. How many target members benefit?
3. Does it solve an observed or assumed problem?
4. Is the need immediate, recurring, seasonal, or optional?
5. What preparation and follow-up are required?
6. Does it depend on one person?
7. Can a backup perform it using an SOP?
8. Can it be tested in a smaller form?
9. What happened in previous attempts?
10. What evidence would justify continuing or stopping it?
11. Are admins selecting it because members need it or because it is interesting to admins?

### Required Pilot Selection

- Three essential administrative processes.
- One recurring engagement experiment.
- One learning event only if a speaker and coordinator explicitly accept ownership.
- A backlog for all other work.

Likely administrative candidates include:

- Member entry and onboarding.
- Message approval and moderation.
- Weekly activity and operations reporting.

Possible engagement candidates include:

- Weekly Python discussion question.
- Weekly quiz.
- Fortnightly coding challenge.

### Capacity Check

```text
Total available capacity = sum of each admin's reliable weekly minutes

Planned workload = sum of estimated weekly minutes for selected activities
```

Use no more than approximately 70% of confirmed capacity. Reserve the remaining 30% for incidents, delays, and
unexpected work.

## Decisions Required During the Meeting

### 1. Three-Month Community Purpose

The purpose statement should identify:

- Target members.
- Problems addressed.
- Practical member value.
- Primary delivery methods.
- Time horizon.
- Major exclusions.

Formula:

```text
For the next three months, [community] will help [primary members]
to [practical outcomes] through [operating methods],
while excluding [major out-of-scope content].
```

Draft example:

> During the next three months, the community will help Python learners and working developers improve practical
> skills, solve technical problems with peers, and discover credible learning and career opportunities through
> moderated weekly activities.

### 2. Minimum Admin Contribution

Decide whether operational admins must provide:

- A minimum reliable weekly time commitment.
- One recurring responsibility or named backup role.
- A routine response-time commitment.
- Advance notice of absence.
- Participation in periodic reviews.

Allow different contribution models:

- Operational owner.
- Backup.
- Occasional contributor.
- Advisor without routine execution.
- Temporarily unavailable admin.

### 3. Individual Availability and Preferred Responsibilities

Record actual availability, preferred areas, unavailable periods, urgent-response ability, required access, and work
the person explicitly cannot own.

### 4. Three SOP Priorities

For every selected SOP, assign:

- Author.
- Reviewer.
- Approver.
- Draft deadline.
- Test date.
- Finalization date.
- Storage location.

Likely first SOPs:

1. Member entry and onboarding.
2. Message approval and moderation.
3. Weekly activity planning, publication, and reporting.

### 5. One Engagement Pilot

Define the activity, target members, frequency, owner, backup, preparation time, publication dates, participation
target, feedback method, and continue, improve, and stop thresholds.

### 6. Owner and Backup for Every Activity

The backup needs a takeover trigger and sufficient documentation and access to perform the process.

### 7. Communication Channel and Response Targets

Define separate targets for routine messages, scheduled decisions, urgent safety matters, and non-urgent ideas.

### 8. Routine Approvals and Urgent Moderation

For routine approvals, define:

- Categories requiring approval.
- Review frequency.
- Approval target.
- Approve, reject, revise, and escalate criteria.
- Rejection communication.
- Second-review requirements.
- Decision record.

For urgent incidents, define:

- What qualifies as urgent.
- Who may remove harmful content immediately.
- Who may restrict or remove a member.
- When second-admin approval is required.
- How evidence is preserved.
- How the affected person is contacted.
- How appeals and post-incident reviews work.

Suggested severity model:

| Severity | Example | Initial action | Authority | Target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low | Off-topic post | Remind or remove | Moderation owner | 24 hours |
| Medium | Repeated promotion | Warn and record | Owner or backup | 12 hours |
| High | Harassment or scam | Remove content and restrict | Any available admin | As soon as seen |
| Critical | Threat or compromised account | Contain and escalate | Available admin and lead | Immediate |

### 9. Metrics, Reporting, and Review Date

For each metric, define its name, exact definition, purpose, baseline, target, collection method, owner, frequency,
storage location, and interpretation.

Recommended rhythm:

- Record activity completion immediately.
- Record participation after each activity.
- Record incidents when they occur.
- Record admin time weekly.
- Produce one weekly summary.
- Conduct the full review after 30 days.

### 10. Repeated Failure to Fulfil Responsibilities

Distinguish:

- One-time personal or professional disruption.
- Failure to communicate.
- Capacity mismatch.
- Skill or access gap.
- Process failure.
- Repeated non-performance.
- Role mismatch.

Recommended policy:

> A missed commitment first triggers a private check-in to identify personal, capacity, process, access, or skill
> constraints. If the same responsibility is missed twice during the pilot without advance communication, ownership
> is reviewed and may be reassigned. Role changes are operational decisions rather than personal judgments.

### 11. Need for Additional Admins or Volunteers

Base recruitment on uncovered work rather than general dissatisfaction.

Ask:

1. Is the gap caused by insufficient people or inefficient work?
2. Can low-value work be removed?
3. Can frequency or scope be reduced?
4. Can an SOP let an existing admin help?
5. Can a trusted member volunteer without admin privileges?
6. Is the missing role an admin, moderator, event coordinator, or content volunteer?
7. What exact responsibility, skill, time window, and weekly effort are missing?
8. What access would the person require?
9. Should a 30-day probation period apply?
10. Who trains and reviews the person?

### 12. Automation Backlog

For each idea, record the manual process, owner, frequency, current effort, inputs, outputs, decision points,
exceptions, human gates, expected saving, risks, possible maintainer, and earliest evaluation date.

Do not start automation development during this meeting.

## Detailed Meeting Outcomes

### 1. Clear Community Purpose

The statement must identify the target members, the value provided, the delivery methods, and the immediate time
horizon. It should be specific enough to reject unrelated work.

Quality questions:

1. Does it identify whom the community serves?
2. Does it describe an observable benefit?
3. Can the community act on it?
4. Can progress be observed within three months?
5. Does it exclude irrelevant activity?
6. Is it realistic with current capacity?

### 2. Complete First-Pass Activity Inventory

"Complete" means all currently known work is visible across membership, moderation, communication, engagement,
learning, opportunities, governance, measurement, and automation candidates.

Every activity needs at least a name, purpose, current status, current owner, frequency, effort estimate, omission
risk, SOP need, and selection status.

### 3. Honest Weekly Capacity

Record reliable time, days, response ability, preferences, limitations, and whether the person is an owner, backup,
occasional contributor, advisor, or temporarily unavailable.

Each attendee should confirm:

> I can reliably contribute `[time]` per week during the pilot, mainly for `[responsibilities]`.

### 4. Named Owners and Backups

Every selected activity needs one owner, one backup where needed, a takeover trigger, shared access, an SOP or
checklist, a time budget, and completion evidence.

No selected row should contain `TBD`, `all admins`, `anyone available`, or `decide later`.

### 5. 30-Day Calendar With Dates

Include the pilot start and end, engagement dates, event dates, announcements, reminders, SOP deadlines, weekly
reports, midpoint check, and final review.

Each event should show what happens, who owns it, when preparation is due, and what evidence proves completion.

### 6. Three SOPs With Authors and Deadlines

Each SOP should define purpose, scope, trigger, inputs, role, procedure, decision criteria, exceptions, escalation,
response target, completion record, metrics, and review ownership.

The backup should be able to perform the process without asking for undocumented private knowledge.

### 7. Success and Failure Metrics

Every pilot activity requires three thresholds:

- Continue.
- Improve and retest.
- Stop.

Defining failure prevents low-value activities from becoming permanent and prevents automation from preserving a bad
process.

### 8. Review Meeting Date

Record the date, time, required attendees, report deadline, evidence required, and decisions to be made.

### 9. Owned Unresolved-Issues List

Every unresolved item needs a specific question, missing information, owner, deadline, decision date, and interim
operating assumption.

Avoid notes such as "discuss later", "pending", or "someone should investigate".

## Detailed Metrics

### Active Contributors

Definition: members making a meaningful contribution, such as asking a relevant question, providing a useful answer,
attempting a challenge, sharing a project, participating substantively in a session, or helping another member.

Do not automatically count greetings, reactions, repeated forwards, unrelated posts, or administrative reminders.

Use it to assess participation breadth, member contribution, admin dependency, and whether activity is concentrated in
a small core.

### Unique Participants

Definition: distinct members participating in an activity, regardless of message volume. One member posting 20
messages still counts as one unique participant.

Segment when useful by first-time participants, returning participants, intended audience, and admins versus members.

### Event Attendance

Separate registrations, confirmed registrations, actual attendees, peak attendance, midpoint attendance, end
attendance, and no-shows.

```text
Attendance rate = actual attendees / registrations x 100

Completion rate = end attendees / actual attendees x 100
```

Use these measures to understand topic relevance, promotion, reminders, timing, and session retention.

### Response Time

Track different workflows separately:

- Membership-request review.
- Post approval.
- Admin-message acknowledgement.
- Member-report acknowledgement.
- Serious-incident containment.
- Technical-question response where relevant.

Prefer median routine response time, maximum urgent response time, and percentage completed within target. Do not
promise continuous admin availability.

### Moderation Incidents

Categorize spam, unapproved promotion, scams, off-topic content, personal attacks, harassment, misinformation,
privacy violations, repeated violations, and admin-related complaints.

Record date, category, severity, initial action, resolution, response time, repeat status, SOP sufficiency, and admin
effort.

### Activity Completion Rate

```text
Completion rate = completed activities / planned activities x 100
```

Use statuses: completed on time, completed late, partially completed, cancelled with notice, and missed without
notice.

Use the result to evaluate planning realism and process reliability, not merely individual performance.

### Admin Time Spent

Track preparation, delivery, moderation, communication, follow-up, reporting, incident handling, and automation
maintenance separately where useful.

Use rough time bands to keep reporting light:

- Under 5 minutes.
- 5-15 minutes.
- 16-30 minutes.
- 31-60 minutes.
- Over 60 minutes.

Compare estimated with actual time and consider admin minutes per meaningful participant when comparing activities.

### Member Feedback

Use short polls, three-question forms, periodic interviews, open feedback, or private feedback.

Recommended questions:

1. How useful was the activity from 1 to 5?
2. What was specifically useful?
3. What should change?
4. Would you participate again?
5. What action will you take afterward?
6. What support do you need next?

Distinguish enjoyment, usefulness, learning, application, and willingness to return.

### Repeat Participation

```text
Repeat participation rate = returning participants / eligible previous participants x 100
```

Use it to assess sustained value and trust. Investigate low return before judging because some activities are useful
only once or may have timing and targeting problems.

### Practical Outcomes

Record voluntary, non-sensitive outcomes such as questions resolved, bugs fixed, projects completed, practices
applied, interviews obtained, collaborations formed, open-source contributions, mentoring, member-led sessions, and
reusable resources.

Do not require disclosure of salary, private employment details, identity, or other unnecessary personal information.

### Why Message Count Is Insufficient

High message volume can result from greetings, forwards, arguments, spam, a few dominant members, reminders, or
unfocused conversations. A quieter group can create greater value through accurate answers, credible opportunities,
learning, collaboration, mentoring, and successful problem resolution.

Use message count only as context alongside unique participants, meaningful contributors, repeat participation,
feedback, practical outcomes, and admin effort.

## Model 30-Day Pilot

### Administrative Process 1: Membership and Onboarding

- Frequency: fixed daily or weekly review window.
- Suggested time budget: 15-20 minutes per week, adjusted after measurement.
- Deliverable: requests reviewed consistently; accepted members receive rules and welcome information.
- Evidence: volume, processing time, exceptions, and admin effort.
- SOP: eligibility, approval, rejection, welcome, and escalation.
- Success: at least 90% processed within the agreed target.

### Administrative Process 2: Moderation

- Frequency: review at agreed intervals, plus urgent action as incidents are observed.
- Suggested time budget: 30 minutes per week plus genuine exceptions.
- Deliverable: posts reviewed consistently and incidents recorded.
- Evidence: number, category, response time, result, and effort.
- SOP: approve, reject, request revision, warn, remove, and escalate.
- Success: routine and urgent response targets are met without unsustainable workload.

### Administrative Process 3: Weekly Operations Review

- Frequency: weekly.
- Suggested time budget: 15-20 minutes.
- Deliverable: compact report of completion, effort, participation, incidents, feedback, and blockers.
- Success: four reports completed within the time budget.

### Engagement Experiment: Weekly Python Prompt

- Frequency: weekly for four weeks.
- Formats: question, debugging scenario, quiz, or short coding challenge.
- Suggested time budget: no more than 30 minutes per activity.
- Evidence: unique, first-time, and repeat participants; response quality; effort; feedback.
- Example continue threshold: average of at least 10 meaningful participants with sustainable preparation.
- Example improve threshold: five to nine participants with useful feedback.
- Example stop threshold: fewer than five participants after repeated tests and no clear learning value.

### Optional Learning Event

Include it only when there is a confirmed speaker, coordinator, topic, outcome, date, platform, promotion owner,
reminder owner, fallback, preparation budget, attendance target, and feedback method.

## Difficult-Moment Facilitation

### Personal Blame

> Let us translate that into an observable operational problem. Which responsibility was not completed, what impact
> did that have, and what process would prevent recurrence?

### "Everyone Should Help"

> Everyone can support it, but who is accountable for starting it, checking completion, and reporting the result?

### Over-Commitment

> Your confirmed capacity is 30 minutes, while these assignments total about 90 minutes. Which one will you own, and
> which should be reassigned or deferred?

### No Volunteer

Ask whether the activity is essential, can be reduced, can be deferred, can use a member volunteer, or creates a
specific recruitment requirement. An activity without an owner is not selected.

### Premature Automation

> I am recording that in the automation backlog. Before implementation, we need the manual steps, exceptions,
> frequency, current effort, and human approvals.

### Large Event Proposal

Ask for the coordinator, speaker, outcome, date, preparation estimate, promotion owner, fallback, and which existing
activity will be removed to create capacity.

### Disagreement

Determine whether the disagreement concerns facts, priorities, risk tolerance, capacity, or authority. Ask whether a
lower-risk option can be tested reversibly for 30 days. Record minority concerns.

### Running Out of Time

Protect these decisions in order: purpose, capacity, pilot scope, owners, dates, metrics, and review date. Move other
details into owned unresolved issues.

## Moderator Control Phrases

### Request Evidence

- Can you give one recent example?
- How frequently has that happened?
- Is that based on data, member feedback, or an assumption?
- What was the operational impact?

### Clarify Language

- What does "active" mean in measurable terms?
- What would "better moderation" look like operationally?
- Which day and frequency does "regularly" mean?
- When you say "we", who is accountable?

### Control Scope

- That is valuable, but outside the minimum pilot. I am placing it in the backlog.
- Which selected activity should we remove if we add this?
- Can this wait until the 30-day review?

### Manage Long Answers

- I will summarize the main point to protect everyone's time.
- I heard the problem as `[summary]`. Is that correct?
- What decision do you need from the group?

### Resolve Vagueness

- What is the exact deliverable?
- What date should we record?
- What evidence proves completion?
- What is the maximum time commitment?

### Handle Silence

- This decision needs explicit agreement, so I will wait a few seconds.
- If nobody can own it, it remains in the backlog.
- Does the silence mean agreement, uncertainty, or lack of capacity?

## Three-State Activity Evaluation

### Continue

Use when the activity creates visible member value, delivery is reliable, effort stays within budget, risk is
manageable, and participation or outcomes meet expectations.

### Improve and Retest

Use when some value exists but timing, topic, format, promotion, ownership, effort, or measurement requires change.
Change only one or two variables before retesting.

### Stop

Use when value remains weak, participation remains low, effort is disproportionate, no sustainable owner exists, a
better external option exists, or risks are unacceptable. Stopping is a valid learning outcome.

## Member Value Ladder

1. Exposure: member sees content.
2. Participation: member responds, attempts, or attends.
3. Understanding: member explains, completes, or asks a deeper question.
4. Application: member solves a problem, improves a project, or acts on an opportunity.
5. Contribution: member helps others, teaches, mentors, organizes, or creates a reusable resource.

For every activity, ask which level it is designed to produce.

## 15-Day Midpoint Review

### Execution

1. Which activities occurred on time?
2. Which were late, cancelled, or missed?
3. Did backups work?
4. Was evidence recorded?
5. Is ownership still clear?

### Capacity

1. What did each activity actually require?
2. Which estimates were wrong?
3. Is work concentrated on one person?
4. Is any commitment unsustainable?
5. Should scope or frequency decrease?

### Member Value

1. Which activities produced meaningful participation?
2. Did they reach intended members?
3. Did members return?
4. What feedback and practical outcomes appeared?

### Operations

1. Did moderation targets work?
2. Which cases were difficult?
3. Can backups use the SOPs?
4. What exceptions were discovered?
5. Are communication channels working?

### Decisions

Decide what continues unchanged, needs one adjustment, stops, remains inconclusive, or requires urgent addition.
Avoid expanding scope unless capacity is clearly available.

## 30-Day Final Review

### Plan Versus Execution

- Compare planned, completed, late, partial, cancelled, and missed activities.
- Identify personal, procedural, technical, access, and capacity causes.

### Value

- Identify direct member benefits and supporting evidence.
- Review unique, first-time, and repeat participation.
- Review practical outcomes and feedback.

### Sustainability

- Compare estimated and actual time.
- Identify hidden preparation and follow-up.
- Confirm whether owners and backups can continue.
- Identify private-knowledge dependencies.

### Governance

- Review documentation, communication, response targets, missed commitments, disagreements, and role gaps.

### Automation

- Identify stable and frequent processes.
- Separate deterministic steps from human judgment.
- Estimate time savings and maintenance.
- Review platform, privacy, and security risks.
- Define required human gates.

### Final Dispositions

- Continue.
- Improve and retest.
- Automate partially.
- Delegate.
- Pause.
- Stop.
